# Slide-by-Slide Analysis: Team Mythos — Accenture B-School Challenge Winning Deck

## Purpose of this document

This document analyses the attached **Team Mythos | Indian School of Business — Accenture B-School Challenge, Decade Edition** presentation. It is intended to be attached to a separate Claude conversation as a reference for storytelling, slide architecture, animation strategy, and presentation craft.

The aim is **not to copy the case content, industry, visual identity, or specific solution**. The aim is to understand the techniques that make the presentation persuasive and how those techniques can be adapted to a different business challenge.

### Important inspection note

The source is a 7-slide PowerPoint with embedded media and extensive native PowerPoint animation sequences. Animation descriptions below are based on the presentation's animation metadata and the intended narrative implied by its objects and speaker notes. A PDF/rendered slide image does not preserve click-by-click animation states; some animated elements overlap or appear simultaneously in static exports. Therefore, where the exact playback sequence cannot be confidently established from a static render, this analysis describes the evident animation strategy rather than claiming an exact sequence. The live PowerPoint should be played in Slide Show mode to verify precise timing and triggers.

# 1. The deck's central storytelling idea

The deck is built around one memorable thesis:

> **“THE CUSTOMER DIDN’T DISAPPEAR. OWNERSHIP DID.”**

That line is the presentation's organising principle, not merely a title. It reframes the problem from a broad customer-experience or technology problem into a specific organisational failure: no one owns the customer journey across disconnected moments.

The narrative then moves through a deliberate chain:

1. **Business contradiction:** Asteron has scale and product quality, yet its customer experience and market position are deteriorating.
2. **Root-cause reveal:** The company has improved individual steps, but the dealer—the customer control point—creates a break in ownership.
3. **Operating-model answer:** Four controls—context, owner, decision, outcome—close the loop.
4. **Proof through experience:** A concrete interface makes the abstract operating model tangible.
5. **Enterprise transformation:** The proposed change can scale from fragmented systems to an AI-enabled enterprise, with human accountability preserved.
6. **Capital-allocation decision:** The team makes a specific budget decision rather than presenting transformation as an unlimited wish list.
7. **Emotional and commercial close:** Financial value is tied back to Priya, the customer who was previously lost.

The key lesson is that each slide changes the audience's understanding of the problem or strengthens the case for action. The deck is not simply a collection of analyses.

# 2. Slide-by-slide analysis

## Slide 1 — Cover: “The customer didn’t disappear. Ownership did.”

### Role in the story
Establishes the presentation's identity and introduces the central thesis before the audience has seen the supporting evidence.

### What is on the slide
- Accenture B-School Challenge branding and “Decade Edition.”
- The case-study descriptor.
- Team Mythos and Indian School of Business attribution.
- Four team-member portraits.
- A large, high-contrast visual built around the number “10” and a woman looking at her phone.
- The headline is treated as the intellectual hook rather than the event name alone.

### Why it works
- **It opens with an argument.** The headline already contains a point of view: the customer is not necessarily lost because of product or price; the failure may be ownership.
- **It creates curiosity.** The audience naturally wants to know what “ownership” means and where it broke.
- **It uses a human visual.** The customer image makes an enterprise transformation problem feel connected to a real person.
- **It separates the thesis from credentials.** Team and competition details are present, but they do not dominate the conceptual hook.

### Animation and media strategy
- The slide contains an embedded video and native timing instructions, including a play command. It is designed as a cinematic opening rather than a completely static title page.
- Elements are controlled through a click-based sequence; the video is not simply decorative background imagery.
- The visual treatment uses a strong, high-contrast composition that can establish mood before the substantive story begins.

**What to learn:** Use motion only if it establishes the problem's emotional context or focuses attention. The opening should still communicate its core thesis if the video fails to play.

### How to adapt the technique
For another business case, open with one sharp, defensible assertion that expresses the central tension. Avoid starting with “Our solution” or a generic problem statement. If a short video is used, it should establish the customer or business situation—not delay the argument.

### Watch-outs
- Do not let a cinematic cover consume too much presentation time.
- Ensure the headline remains readable and meaningful without animation.
- Do not use a customer image as a substitute for evidence.

## Slide 2 — Business contradiction: “Asteron should be winning”

### Role in the story
Creates the gap between what should be happening and what is actually happening. It establishes the reason the business needs intervention.

### What is on the slide
A central Asteron vehicle image is surrounded by four evidence clusters:

- **Product quality:** 82/100, with a portfolio spanning hatchbacks, sedans, SUVs, and EVs.
- **Scale:** ₹74,000 crore FY2026 revenue and 1,050 dealer outlets.
- **Customer experience:** NPS of 24 against a competitor benchmark of 39.
- **Market position:** rank falling from #3 to #4 and market share falling from 13.4% to 11.2%.

The headline says the company *should* be winning; the metrics show why that expectation is contradicted by reality.

### Why it works
- **It makes the problem legible quickly.** Four evidence groups are enough to establish scale, product strength, experience weakness, and commercial decline.
- **It uses contrast rather than a data dump.** Product quality and company scale sit against weak NPS and falling market position.
- **It creates a question that the rest of the deck must answer:** if the product and scale are not the issue, what is?
- **The central image acts as an anchor.** The surrounding metrics feel like different dimensions of the same company, not unrelated facts.

### Animation strategy
The slide contains multiple native entrance effects, including fades, wipes, and a wheel-style reveal. Some elements have staggered delays. The apparent strategy is to reveal the business picture progressively rather than present every data point at once.

A strong delivery sequence would be:
1. Establish the company and product quality.
2. Reveal scale.
3. Introduce the customer-experience deficit.
4. Finish with the declining rank and market share.

The last reveal should carry the greatest weight because it is the business consequence of the contradiction.

### How it advances the story
It converts the opening assertion into a business problem. The audience now knows that the company's underperformance is not self-evidently explained by weak products or limited reach.

### How to adapt the technique
Use a small number of high-signal metrics to establish a contradiction. Avoid filling the slide with every market statistic available. Make sure at least one metric shows the business consequence, not only the symptoms.

### Watch-outs
- Each metric needs a source, period, and definition in a real submission.
- Avoid implying causality from correlation. These metrics establish a problem, not yet its root cause.
- A central visual should not make labels or numbers too small.

## Slide 3 — Root-cause reveal: “The dealer is the customer control point. The loop breaks around it.”

### Role in the story
Moves from “what is going wrong?” to “where does the system fail?” This is the main diagnostic slide.

### What is on the slide
A circular customer journey places the dealer at the centre and maps interactions between Asteron and the customer.

- Asteron-side inputs include vehicle supply, demand signals, and offer rules.
- Customer-side commitments include callback, test drive, delivery, and service.
- Dealer-related interventions include lead scoring, digital scheduling, dealer training, and a service dashboard.
- The slide asks four diagnostic questions:
  - What do we know?
  - Who owns it?
  - What can we promise?
  - Was it closed?
- The diagram highlights untracked and unresolved customer moments.
- The headline resolves into: **“Asteron improved each step. The journey still breaks.”**

### Why it works
- **It shifts from symptoms to mechanism.** The audience can see how separate improvements can coexist with a broken end-to-end journey.
- **The dealer is positioned as a control point.** This makes the failure specific and actionable rather than blaming “poor customer experience” in general.
- **The circular diagram implies continuity.** The intended customer journey should be a loop, but missing ownership prevents closure.
- **The questions act as a diagnostic framework.** They create a reusable way to identify journey failures.

### Animation strategy
This slide has an especially dense native animation system: fades, wipes, colour emphasis, motion, and scaling, distributed across many objects and multiple click effects. That is consistent with a staged diagnostic reveal.

The intended technique appears to be **progressive construction of the journey**:
1. Introduce the system and its actors.
2. Build the journey around the dealer.
3. Surface the disconnected handoffs and untracked moments.
4. Reveal the diagnostic questions and unresolved counts.
5. Land on the final assertion that local improvements have not fixed the whole journey.

The headline itself appears to have an initial and final state, so the conclusion can be delivered after the visual diagnosis rather than all at once.

### Why the animation matters
Without progressive builds, the diagram could feel overly dense. Animation can direct the audience's attention to one part of the system at a time and allow the speaker to explain cause and effect.

### How it advances the story
It provides the causal bridge between the business contradiction on Slide 2 and the proposed operating model on Slide 4.

### How to adapt the technique
Use a system diagram to show where the process breaks, who controls the critical handoff, and what is not being recorded or resolved. Then turn the root cause into a clear sentence.

### Watch-outs
- This slide is visually dense. It needs a rehearsed explanation and disciplined animation pacing.
- A viewer who sees the slide as a static PDF may not understand every line. Include a simplified, legible static version in any PDF submission.
- Do not animate every label individually just because the software allows it. Each reveal must support the causal argument.
- Counts such as “untracked” and “unresolved” should be clearly defined and traceable to the case evidence.

## Slide 4 — Solution logic: “Give every customer moment an owner”

### Role in the story
Presents the operating principle that resolves the diagnosed failure. It is the conceptual heart of the proposed solution.

### What is on the slide
The subtitle states: **“Four controls close the customer journey.”**

The four controls are:
1. **Context** — one customer state.
2. **Owner** — a person and deadline.
3. **Decision** — the right level of support.
4. **Outcome** — result recorded.

The slide also distinguishes what should be handled by:
- Clear, repeatable rules and processes.
- AI-assisted, pattern-based prediction and recommendation.
- Consequential human decisions, such as approving, changing, or rejecting a final offer or delivery commitment.

It states the governance principle explicitly:

> Rules define what is allowed. AI suggests what may work. People own the promise.

### Why it works
- **It turns a diagnosis into a control model.** Instead of saying “integrate systems” or “use AI,” it specifies what must be true at each customer moment.
- **It separates roles.** Rules establish boundaries, AI supports judgement, and a person remains accountable for consequential promises.
- **It makes the solution operational.** “Owner + deadline” and “outcome recorded” are behaviours and controls, not abstract aspirations.
- **It connects customer experience with governance.** The solution is not simply more automation; it is reliable closure with accountable ownership.

### Animation strategy
The slide contains many fades, scaling effects, motion paths, colour emphasis, and wipe effects. It appears designed to build the control model in stages and then reveal the division of responsibility.

A clear presentation sequence would be:
1. Show the customer journey/control point.
2. Introduce the four controls in order: context, owner, decision, outcome.
3. Show where rules operate.
4. Show where AI recommends.
5. Highlight the human decision point.
6. Finish with the governance principle.

This creates a progression from **what the system must remember** to **who must act** to **how the decision is supported** to **how closure is confirmed**.

### How it advances the story
It answers the “what changes?” question raised by Slide 3 and defines the logic that the product interface on Slide 5 must make real.

### How to adapt the technique
Present the solution as a small number of operating principles or controls. Make each one observable and testable. Where AI is involved, specify what it can recommend, what it may execute, and what requires human approval.

### Watch-outs
- The slide can become hard to follow if the four controls and AI architecture are explained simultaneously.
- Do not allow abstract terms such as “AI-assisted” or “agentic” to obscure the actual workflow.
- The four controls should be visually dominant; supporting AI detail should remain secondary.

## Slide 5 — Tangible proof: “This time Priya doesn’t get lost”

### Role in the story
Makes the operating model concrete. The audience can see what a frontline user might actually use and how the new process changes day-to-day work.

### What is on the slide
A large interface mock-up called **Asteron LOOP** shows a “My Handoffs” workspace. It includes:
- Customer names and journey context.
- Enquiry, walk-in, service, test-drive, exchange, and follow-up items.
- Visible exceptions such as callback overdue, no follow-up record, waiting for a part, no valuation given, or no outcome recorded.
- A named owner for a handoff.
- A right-side tracker showing unresolved and untracked moments, customer moments, and recorded outcomes.
- A specific customer, Priya Sharma, whose enquiry has a callback overdue.

The headline connects the product to a person: **“This time Priya doesn’t get lost.”**

### Why it works
- **It moves from concept to experience.** The previous slide described controls; this slide shows how those controls might appear in a real workflow.
- **It makes the problem visible.** Unresolved handoffs are not buried in a process document; they appear as actionable work.
- **It shows ownership in the interface.** Named owners and overdue items make accountability concrete.
- **It provides an emotional bridge.** Priya is not just a generic customer; her journey gives the story a human thread.
- **It increases perceived feasibility.** A plausible interface helps the audience imagine the solution being adopted by a dealer or manager.

### Animation and media strategy
The slide contains numerous fades, colour-emphasis effects, scaling, motion, wipes, and an embedded media element. The animation system is substantial, suggesting that the interface or related visual sequence is meant to be revealed progressively.

A useful delivery pattern is:
1. Establish the handoff dashboard.
2. Highlight an unresolved customer moment.
3. Reveal the owner or deadline.
4. Show how the status or outcome becomes visible to the manager.
5. Return attention to Priya and the promise that she will not disappear into an unowned handoff.

The precise role and playback timing of the embedded media should be checked in PowerPoint Slide Show mode. Do not assume a static PDF accurately captures the live sequence.

### How it advances the story
It demonstrates how the conceptual solution becomes a frontline workflow. It also makes the next step—enterprise-wide adoption and scaling—more credible.

### How to adapt the technique
For a product proposal, include a meaningful prototype or workflow visual. Show the specific user action, the system response, and the outcome. A polished mock-up is useful only if it proves the mechanism, not merely the aesthetics.

### Watch-outs
- The interface should be presented as a proposed or illustrative design unless it is a functioning, validated product.
- Avoid displaying unnecessary fake operational details as if they were real evidence.
- Ensure the audience can read the key interface elements from a distance.
- Explain one representative interaction instead of trying to narrate every row.

## Slide 6 — Scale and roadmap: “From fragmented systems to enterprise AI”

### Role in the story
Shows how the solution can extend beyond a single interface into a staged enterprise transformation. It addresses scalability without pretending that the organisation can jump directly to full autonomy.

### What is on the slide
A left-to-right progression presents five stages:

1. **Disconnected enterprise — Today:** data, pilots, and vendors sit apart; 32 pilots and 11 vendors; no shared customer record.
2. **Connected enterprise:** one customer foundation, shared record, common rules, clear owner.
3. **AI-assisted enterprise:** AI suggests, people decide.
4. **Agentic enterprise:** work moves across sales, service, and stock; one task, no new system.
5. **AI-native enterprise — Year 3:** approved journeys learn and improve, with common controls and people accountable.

The illustrations progress from disconnected data and systems to coordinated enterprise workflows and a customer-facing outcome.

### Why it works
- **It shows progression rather than a big-bang transformation.** The future state is decomposed into capabilities that can be developed over time.
- **It gives the audience a maturity model.** The sequence explains what must exist before more advanced automation becomes sensible.
- **It preserves human accountability.** Even as the enterprise becomes more AI-enabled, common controls and people remain accountable.
- **The illustration reinforces the journey.** The visual moves across the page, making the roadmap easy to scan.

### Animation strategy
The slide uses sequential wipes, fades, colour emphasis, motion, and scaling. The native animation structure supports a left-to-right build.

A strong reveal sequence would be:
1. Show the fragmented current state.
2. Add the shared foundation.
3. Introduce AI assistance.
4. Reveal cross-functional/agentic execution.
5. Finish with the year-three AI-native state and the control principles.

This sequence makes dependencies visible: shared data and rules come before higher autonomy.

### How it advances the story
It answers “Can this work beyond one dealer or one workflow?” while preserving a staged, governed path to scale.

### How to adapt the technique
Use a capability roadmap with clear stage names, prerequisites, and decision gates. Show how each stage builds on the previous one, and distinguish the near-term deliverable from the long-term ambition.

### Watch-outs
- The roadmap should not imply that all stages will happen automatically.
- Each stage needs explicit prerequisites, owners, timing, and evidence gates in a real implementation plan.
- Terms such as “agentic” and “AI-native” should be defined in plain language or kept secondary to operational outcomes.
- The illustration is attractive, but the milestones must remain readable and measurable.

## Slide 7 — Investment and value case: “Project Vega — 6 months earlier”

### Role in the story
Turns the recommendation into a concrete resource-allocation decision and closes the argument with financial and human outcomes.

### What is on the slide
The slide begins with an investment envelope:
- ₹250 crore annually over three years.
- ₹750 crore total budget.

It then distinguishes:
- **Continue — ₹690 crore:** platform, priority use cases, dealer integration, workforce training, and contingency/Phase 2.
- **Redirect — ₹60 crore:** Vega launch readiness.
- **Pause:** personalised outbound offers, unproven pilots, and AI-made customer promises.

It highlights **420 EV-ready outlets on day one** and a value case of **₹1,826 crore gross value created**, with rounded stakeholder components:
- ₹1,405 crore for Asteron.
- ₹267 crore for dealers.
- ₹153 crore for customers.

The slide also calls out approximately 3× ROIC, +130 basis points of margin, and improved customer choice/ownership experience. The speaker notes explain the financial logic and close by returning to Priya: she walked away because nobody made her feel that she mattered.

### Why it works
- **It makes the recommendation specific.** The team is not asking for unlimited funding; it is reallocating ₹60 crore while protecting the broader transformation investment.
- **It combines continue, redirect, and pause.** This is more executive-ready than a list of initiatives because it demonstrates trade-offs.
- **It connects investment to value.** The slide shows both financial outputs and stakeholder benefits.
- **It distinguishes value creation from allocation.** The speaker notes clarify that dealer and customer benefits are economic benefits, not cash payouts.
- **It closes the emotional loop.** Priya, introduced as a lost customer, becomes the human meaning behind the financial results.

### Animation and media strategy
This is the most animation-heavy slide in the deck. The PowerPoint contains a large number of fade effects, some motion and scaling, colour emphasis, wipes, and an embedded video with play/pause commands. The notes provide a clear verbal order:

1. Establish the ₹750 crore three-year budget envelope.
2. Show the ₹690 crore retained for platform and priority investments.
3. Show the ₹60 crore redirected to Vega readiness.
4. Reveal the initiatives that pause.
5. Highlight 420 EV-ready outlets on day one.
6. Reveal gross value and stakeholder components.
7. Land on ROIC, margin, and customer outcomes.
8. Close with Priya and the central ownership thesis.

The embedded media and animation metadata suggest a multimedia closing sequence, but the exact playback order and trigger should be verified in PowerPoint. The static PDF export shows a video frame rather than the full financial composition, so it is not a reliable representation of the final slide's live playback state.

### Why it works as a close
It answers three executive questions:
- **What are you asking us to do?** Reallocate ₹60 crore to readiness.
- **What do we get?** A quantified value case and operational outcomes.
- **Why does it matter?** It helps the company keep the customer promise that the earlier journey failed to deliver.

### How to adapt the technique
Finish with a decision, not a summary. State what to fund, what to stop or defer, what outcomes to expect, and how the customer or business will be different. Bring the closing back to the opening idea so the presentation feels complete.

### Watch-outs
- The value figures must be traceable to a model, with assumptions and calculation logic available in the annexure.
- Gross value, NPV, stakeholder benefits, cash flows, and return measures must not be treated as interchangeable.
- Rounded components may not sum exactly to the total; explain rounding or reconciliation.
- A financial slide should remain understandable without animation. Keep the final static state complete and legible.
- Emotional closure should reinforce the business case, not replace the evidence.

# 4. Animation analysis across the deck

## What the animation system is trying to accomplish

The deck uses animation primarily for **narrative sequencing**, not just visual decoration. Its most useful animation roles are:

1. **Progressive disclosure:** reveal a complex diagram or set of metrics in manageable chunks.
2. **Attention direction:** highlight one number, actor, handoff, or decision at the moment the speaker discusses it.
3. **Causal explanation:** build the journey in sequence so the audience sees where it breaks.
4. **Concept-to-proof transition:** move from a conceptual operating model to a concrete interface.
5. **Roadmap progression:** construct a maturity model from left to right.
6. **Financial reveal:** establish the budget, show the reallocation, then reveal value and outcomes.
7. **Emotional reinforcement:** use embedded media and the recurring customer story to make the final recommendation memorable.

## Observed animation profile

The PowerPoint contains native timing and animation metadata on all seven slides. The animation XML includes fades, directional wipes, colour-emphasis effects, motion, scaling, and embedded-media commands. Slides 3, 4, and 7 have particularly extensive animation systems, consistent with their roles as the core diagnosis, solution logic, and business-case reveal.

| Slide | Evident animation/media approach | Narrative purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Click-controlled elements and embedded video playback | Establish the opening mood and thesis |
| 2 | Staggered fades/wipes and a wheel-style entrance | Build the business contradiction in stages |
| 3 | Extensive fades, colour emphasis, motion, scaling, directional wipes | Construct the journey and reveal the break in ownership |
| 4 | Extensive fades, scaling, motion, colour emphasis, wipes | Build the four controls and distinguish rules, AI, and human judgement |
| 5 | Fades, colour emphasis, scaling, motion, wipes, embedded media | Reveal a tangible interface and make the customer journey personal |
| 6 | Sequential wipes/fades, colour emphasis, motion, scaling | Move through the enterprise maturity stages |
| 7 | Extensive fades, motion, scaling, wipes, colour emphasis, embedded video | Sequence the investment decision and value case, then return to the customer |

## What to emulate

- Reveal a conclusion only after the evidence needed to understand it has appeared.
- Build diagrams in the order the audience should read them.
- Use a small number of memorable animation moments rather than animating every object.
- Use consistent transitions so the deck feels like one system.
- Keep the final state of each slide self-explanatory.

## What not to copy blindly

- **Animation volume:** the deck contains very many object-level effects. This is demanding to build, rehearse, and maintain.
- **Dense diagrams:** animation can help live delivery, but cannot fully rescue an overcomplicated slide.
- **Video dependence:** embedded media can fail, delay the presentation, or render differently across systems.
- **Static-export risk:** animation-heavy slides can appear incomplete or overlapped in a PDF export.

For a new presentation, use purposeful builds and reserve the most elaborate motion for perhaps three or four moments: the opening hook, the key diagnostic reveal, the product demonstration, and the final decision/value reveal.

# 5. Visual and design system

## Visual language
- Predominantly black/dark background.
- White, uppercase, assertion-led headlines.
- Purple/magenta as the principal highlight colour, with red used for negative or risk-related signals.
- High-contrast numeric callouts.
- Fine-line diagrams and thin connectors.
- A mix of photographic imagery, illustrative system diagrams, and a realistic product interface.
- Small, restrained footer and slide numbering.

## Why this works
- The dark canvas creates a consistent, high-contrast visual identity.
- Purple acts as a repeatable accent that links different slide types.
- Large headlines communicate the conclusion before the audience studies the visual.
- Diagrams explain systems and handoffs; interface mock-ups make the proposed solution feel concrete.
- The deck alternates analytical and human elements rather than relying on one visual mode throughout.

## What to adapt
Adopt the principles—contrast, consistency, assertion-led headlines, deliberate visual hierarchy—not the exact Accenture branding. For a different company or competition, use an appropriate palette and identity. Make charts and diagrams readable from a distance, and use colour consistently to distinguish current state, failure, intervention, and outcome.

# 6. Storytelling techniques worth carrying into another case

## Technique 1 — Lead with a reframe
The opening sentence changes how the problem is interpreted. A good reframe should be both memorable and defensible.

**Adaptation:** Find the strongest contradiction in the evidence and express the underlying cause, not just the visible symptom.

## Technique 2 — Prove the problem before presenting the solution
The deck first establishes business underperformance, then diagnoses why it persists.

**Adaptation:** Resist the temptation to reveal the product too early. Earn the solution with evidence and root-cause logic.

## Technique 3 — Use one central mechanism
The dealer control point and four controls give the story a coherent operating logic.

**Adaptation:** Make the proposed solution understandable through one core mechanism, not a long feature list.

## Technique 4 — Make the solution visible
The LOOP interface demonstrates how the concept changes frontline work.

**Adaptation:** Show a user journey or prototype that proves the intervention is usable and directly connected to the identified friction.

## Technique 5 — Show the path to scale
The five-stage enterprise model explains how the solution could mature.

**Adaptation:** Separate MVP, pilot, scale-up, and long-term vision. Do not imply that a future-state ambition is already implemented.

## Technique 6 — Show trade-offs
The final slide explicitly continues some investments, redirects others, and pauses risky initiatives.

**Adaptation:** Recommendations become more credible when they explain what the team would not do, as well as what it would do.

## Technique 7 — Close with both value and meaning
The business case is quantified, then linked to Priya's experience.

**Adaptation:** End with the decision, expected outcomes, and a concise callback to the customer insight from the opening.

# 7. Implications for adapting this approach to a different business challenge

Use the following as a storytelling blueprint, not as a mandatory slide template:

1. **Opening assertion:** a memorable, evidence-compatible reframe.
2. **Business contradiction:** why current performance is inconsistent with the company's strengths or ambitions.
3. **Root-cause diagnosis:** where the system or customer journey actually breaks.
4. **Strategic principle:** the small number of controls or behaviours that must change.
5. **Product proof:** a concrete experience or workflow that demonstrates the mechanism.
6. **Validation and feasibility:** what is known, what is assumed, and what must be tested.
7. **Adoption and scale:** how the intervention moves from a focused pilot to broader deployment.
8. **Decision and economics:** what to invest in, what to defer, and how value will be measured.
9. **Closing callback:** return to the opening insight and make the requested decision memorable.

If a submission has a strict slide limit, combine adjacent elements instead of dropping the causal logic. A concise deck should still answer: **Why this problem? Why this cause? Why this solution? Why will it work? What value does it create? What should happen next?**
